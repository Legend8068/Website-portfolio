# STRATA — a design philosophy

**The movement:** Strata. Depth as narrative. Every idea in the work exists at a
physical elevation, and understanding is the act of descending through cut paper.

Space is vertical and geological. The composition is not a page but a core sample:
sheets of stock stacked until they become terrain, each layer a stratum with its own
tone, its own shadow, its own reason to exist beneath the one above. Nothing floats
arbitrarily — an element's depth IS its meaning. What is nearest the viewer is
interface; what is deepest is foundation. The eye does not scan, it excavates. Every
aperture cut through the stack is an invitation: look how far down this goes.

Color is bathymetric. Warm cream at the surface where the light lives, falling
through slate blues into navy blacks at the trench floor, resurfacing at the end into
morning stock. One fixed studio light, always top-left, so that every cut edge carries
its shadow like a signature — short and tight for shallow layers, long and soft for
sheets held high off the canvas. Shadow is never decoration; it is measurement. The
palette holds five voices — coral, teal, gold, sky, navy — and lets paper white do the
speaking between them.

Form is razor-cut, never drawn. Edges are pristine; curves are confident single
strokes of the blade. Depth is achieved by stacking, punching, folding — never by
gradient or blur. Where softness appears, it is the softness of a shadow falling on
fiber, meticulously calibrated, not the softness of a tool's default. Repetition builds
meaning the way sediment builds rock: rings inside rings, waves behind waves,
each layer placed with the painstaking care of a master paper engineer who has
done this ten thousand times.

Motion is tectonic and precise. Layers part, sheets slide past the camera, a fold
opens exactly once and means it. Movement exists to reveal stratigraphy — to
prove the world is physically deep — never to entertain for its own sake. The pace
is a slow exhale; the interaction is a hand pressed gently into the stack.

Text is a surveyor's mark. Small, spaced, engraved into a layer rather than printed
over it: depth readings, stratum names, catalogue numbers. One serif voice for
what matters, one geometric voice for the measurements. The work should read
as the product of countless deliberate hours — an artifact from an imaginary
discipline that maps a person the way oceanographers map a trench — and
every alignment, every shadow length, every cut must look labored over by
someone at the absolute top of their craft.
